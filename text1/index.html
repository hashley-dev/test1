Ancient Vietnam, 1st century AD

Tôn Đức Thắng

The sisters who led Vietnam's first revolt against foreign rule
The story

Two sisters, one uprising

A country under occupation

By the first century AD, the region that is now northern Vietnam had been under Chinese administration for close to a hundred and fifty years. Local customs, land rights, and the authority of Vietnamese aristocratic families were steadily pushed aside by officials sent from the Han court. Taxes were heavy, and the old social order — in which women could inherit land, hold authority, and lead households in their own right — was being replaced by an administrative system that had little patience for it.

It was into this pressure that Trưng Trắc and her younger sister Trưng Nhị were born, daughters of a local lord in the Mê Linh region. They were raised in a household that still practiced the old ways, trained, by most accounts, in both martial skill and the running of an estate. Trưng Trắc married Thi Sách, the lord of a neighboring territory, and for a time the sisters' lives followed the pattern expected of the local nobility.

The spark

The turning point came when the Chinese governor of the region, Tô Định, had Thi Sách executed — a move most accounts describe as an attempt to intimidate the remaining Vietnamese nobility into submission. Instead, it did the opposite. Trưng Trắc, joined by her sister, raised a call to arms that spread quickly through the surrounding territories, drawing in other local lords and their followers, many of whom were themselves women commanding their own forces.

In the year 40 AD, the sisters led their combined army against the Chinese garrisons. The campaign moved fast: dozens of towns and strongholds fell in succession, and Tô Định reportedly fled south in retreat. By the end of the campaign, the sisters had taken some sixty-five citadels across the region.

For three years, the land they had fought for was governed as their own — a rare, brief return to self-rule in a century of occupation.

A short-lived kingdom

With the occupation forces driven out, Trưng Trắc was proclaimed queen, ruling jointly with her sister from a capital at Mê Linh. She reduced the tax burden that had driven so much of the resentment against Chinese rule and worked to restore the older, more decentralized system of local governance the nobility had been used to. It was a short reign — roughly three years — but it stood out sharply against the century of administration that came before and after it.

The Han court did not let the loss stand. In 42 AD, a seasoned general, Mã Viện, was sent south with a large, well-supplied army. The Trưng sisters' forces, still built largely around the levies of local lords rather than a standing army, were eventually worn down. According to the most widely told account, rather than be captured, the sisters chose to take their own lives by drowning in the Hát river in 43 AD, bringing the uprising — and their brief kingdom — to an end.

Why they're still remembered

Nearly two thousand years on, the Trưng sisters remain one of the most widely commemorated pairs in Vietnamese history. Temples in their name stand across the country, most prominently at Mê Linh and in Hanoi, and a public holiday — the Hai Bà Trưng Festival, on the sixth day of the second lunar month — is still observed. Streets, schools, and one of Hanoi's central districts carry their name.

Part of what has kept their story alive is what it represents rather than how long it lasted: a rare instance, in a long stretch of foreign administration, of local rule restored by local hands — and led, notably, by women, in a period whose accounts are otherwise dominated by men. Later resistance movements against occupation, centuries apart, would repeatedly invoke their example.
